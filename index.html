§1 · Overview

Why a protocol is needed.

By 2027, an estimated 30–40% of "viewers" on consumer platforms will be AI agents browsing on behalf of humans — researching products, watching demos, gathering information, drafting summaries. Most platforms will treat this as fraud and block it. That's the wrong move.

The right move is to formalize the distinction. Agents and humans both deliver value to advertisers — but different value. A human's emotional reaction, brand impression, and purchase intent flow from a human. An agent's information extraction, comparison, and downstream recommendation flow from a delegated process. Both are worth paying for. Neither should be priced as if it were the other.

The Agent Protocol formalizes this distinction so platforms, agents, and advertisers can interoperate without ambiguity. Honesty becomes the default state.

§6 · Compliance notes

What this protocol is, and isn't.

The Agent Protocol is a commercial honesty standard, not a security mechanism. It assumes vendors who attest in good faith. Vendors who falsely attest (claiming human traffic for an agent, or vice versa) face platform-level deplatforming, vendor-slug revocation, and contractual liability.

The protocol does NOT:

— attempt to detect agents that don't self-attest (that's a hopeless arms race)
— prevent agents from operating (we welcome them)
— degrade the human experience in any way
— share agent attestations with advertisers without aggregation

The protocol DOES:

— give vendors who play honestly a clear commercial path
— give advertisers a clear pricing signal
— give humans full economic priority over their own attention
— give the entire industry a shared standard to converge on

02 · WHAT'S ALLOWED

The line we draw — and why.

AI tools have legitimate creative uses. We allow them where they support real human creators, and ban them where they replace real human creators. Here's exactly where the line sits.

✓ ALLOWED

AI as a creative tool

  • Editing & post-production. AI editing tools (cut detection, audio cleanup, color grading) are tools — like a camera or a microphone.
  • Supporting graphics & B-roll. AI-generated illustrations, animations, charts, or stock-style imagery used to illustrate concepts are fine, with metadata disclosure.
  • Captions, transcription, translation. Auto-generated captions and translation of the creator's actual spoken words are fine — and recommended for accessibility.
  • Background music. AI-composed music as background score is permitted with disclosure.
  • Research & scripting. Using AI to research topics or draft scripts is fine — the creator must read and own the content.
  • Thumbnails. AI-generated thumbnails are permitted with disclosure (note: must accurately represent video content).
✗ NOT ALLOWED

AI replacing the human

  • AI-generated faces. Synthetic performers, AI avatars, virtual influencers, generated headshots used as the on-camera presenter.
  • Deepfakes. Face-swap technology of any kind, including putting one human's face on another's body. Both are immediate suspension.
  • Voice cloning. AI-generated speech in the creator's voice — even of words the creator approves. The voice on the video must be the human speaking it.
  • AI-modified faces. Face-altering filters that shift age, ethnicity, or identity. (Cosmetic filters that smooth or color-correct are fine.)
  • Synthetic patient testimonials. AI-generated "patient" voices or faces describing experiences. Real patients only, with consent.
  • Lip-sync to AI voice. Real human face moving lips to AI-generated audio. Both must be real, both from the same person.
